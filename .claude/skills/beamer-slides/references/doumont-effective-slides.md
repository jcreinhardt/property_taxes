## The three laws of communication

1. **Adapt to your audience.** 2. **Maximize the signal-to-noise ratio.** 3. **Use effective redundancy.**
Information is "what?"; a message is "so what?" — get the audience to pay attention to, understand and act upon a
maximum of messages given constraints.

## Planning and structure (3.0 summary, verbatim phrases)

- Five planning questions: why (purpose), who (audience), what (content), when (time), where (space).
- Opening: attention getter → need → task → **main message** ("the one sentence I want my audience to remember") →
  preview ("a map of the body, ideally three points, max. five"). Body: points with transitions. Closing:
  review → conclusion ("what the above means to the audience") → close.
- Checklist: structure "five main points or fewer (optimally three exactly) and no more than five subpoints, if any";
  preview limited to one level with short labels; structure visible at a glance.

## Slide rules

1. "Do your slides right… or don't do slides at all. Slides are optional; first plan, design… and rehearse."
2. "With each slide, convey **one message (only)**." One slide per subpoint.
3. **The title is the message** (the "so what"), "a complete sentence, with a subject and a verb";
   "no more than **12 words or so**, well-balanced on **up to two lines**, surrounded by sufficient space";
   placed prominently top-left against a wide left margin (lecture; prinzipien reproduces this: title flush against a
   ~33 % left margin).
4. "Is the content of the slide **visual**? Can it be processed in an instant, globally rather than sequentially?"
   — a diagram/graph/table, not a paragraph; the A→D example turns "Client survey (1/3)" + bullets into
   "We should focus our R&D efforts on our product's accuracy" + a five-row sorted bar table.
5. "State the message verbally, then **develop it visually**": announce, then show; builds only to reveal evidence step
   by step, never decorative animation (lecture).
6. "Create slides that make (reasonable) sense on their own, so they are effectively redundant with what you say"
   (law 3) — but they are not a document; slides and handouts are different objects.
7. "Are text items set large enough, including in tables, graphs, etc.?" "Are all colors contrasted enough when
   projected on the big screen?" "Are data prominent (thick lines, etc.) and is the rest unobtrusive?"
8. "Did you **omit whatever adds little** (background, logo, decoration)?" — no logos, no decorative backgrounds, no
   footers; navigation items (slide numbers, section markers), if any, "segregated, as on another background color".
9. "Avoid titles that add little, such as Preview or Outline — or use the title of the talk instead."
   Structure slides must be recognisable at a glance (different layout, colour, or a consistent graphical element).
10. "Finally, **be concise**, both verbally and visually. 'Sometimes add; but oft'ner take away' (Ars poetica)."
    Everything that does not carry the slide's message is noise; remove it.
11. Templates: "a set of rules to help you choose content … and format it once chosen"; a blank white page is his
    template; design starts from content, not from a layout.
12. Few colours: one neutral text colour plus one accent (his decks: black/greys, one orange accent, red for emphasis);
    grid-based layout; large sans type (Lucida Sans in his handouts).
13. Delivery: memorise the outline, not the wording; no filler words; look at everyone; large deliberate gestures;
    stand still. Questions: listen to the whole question, repeat/rephrase, think, answer to the whole audience.

## Graphs (4.0 summary)

Choose the graph by message (comparison, distribution, correlation, evolution) and by data structure; bars start at
zero, dots on a scale need not; both beat pie charts; different units → separate panels with a common horizontal
scale; juxtaposed panels with identical scales for subsets; no separate legend ("a graph to read, not to view"),
no excess tick marks or gridlines; data ink dominant.

## Conflicts with other sources

- Doumont and Alley coincide almost exactly (sentence title ≤ 2 lines, visual evidence, no logos); Doumont adds the
  12-word cap, the one-level preview, and the explicit "three points" structure.
- "No slide numbers/footers" conflicts with Goldsmith-Pinkham, Butts and metropolis (frame number in footline) and with
  seminar practice where questioners cite slide numbers; compromise: small, segregated number.
- Doumont's "slides optional, rehearse first" and Tufte's "handout instead" both put the deck second; Schwabish and
  Goldsmith-Pinkham assume the deck is the talk.
- Doumont allows a short text table as "visual" if it can be grasped globally; Tufte wants the full table on paper.
