# Tabarrok — How to Give a Great Seminar

- Author: Alex Tabarrok (George Mason University)
- Title: "How Not to Give a Bad Seminar / How to Give a Great Seminar" (title slide
  strikes through the first)
- Year: undated PowerPoint (cites Bellemare's 2014 post, so 2014 or later)
- URL used: https://mason.gmu.edu/~atabarro/HowToGiveAGreatSeminar.pptx
  (unzipped; slide XML + speaker notes read)
- Length: 19 slides with notes on 7 of them

## Concrete rules

1. Speaker note on the title: "It takes a great paper to give a great seminar but **no
   one has to give a bad seminar**."
2. **Practice!** "What looks good to the eye often doesn't sound good to the ear." Listen
   to yourself for stumbling blocks, unclear phrasing, missing arguments.
3. **"Never, ever go long! Especially, do not speed up at the end so that you can finish
   everything."** Practice to finish on time.
4. Practice enough to watch the audience and hear yourself without losing the thread;
   attend to cadence and rhythm.
5. Practice = empty room, then a friend or two, then a home-institution talk or friendly
   conference — **before** presenting the job-market paper for a job.
6. **"Practicing a talk is like editing a paper"** (Buchanan: "Writing is editing"). "You
   wouldn't submit a first draft of a paper. Why do you give a first draft of a talk?"
7. **A good seminar is a good story.**
8. **Hook: "You have five minutes to tell your audience why they shouldn't walk out on
   you."** State the question/puzzle; get curiosity on your side — if people believe they
   have a gap in their knowledge that you will fill, they will listen. Hooks differ by
   audience (notes give three: Black Death killed 40% of Europe → consequences for cities;
   why hasn't progress lowered hours more?; effect of police on crime and the endogeneity
   problem).
9. **Organisation of a talk/paper:** Hook → Research question (specific question + value
   added in theory/data/technique, brief antecedents) → Preview of main results
   (optional) → Data → Research design ("where is identification coming from?") →
   Results → Robustness/limitations → The takeaway.
10. Preview example from the notes: "a 10% increase in police reduces crime by 3% ...
    more police would more than pay for itself" — a number plus its implication.
11. **Every slide should have a purpose** — "NOT to remind you what to say" but to
    advance the story and help the audience follow. Test: **"If someone's attention slips
    for 30 seconds can they catch up by looking at your slide?"** Title the slide with its
    purpose.
12. Graphs: labelled; a note on data source and transformations; **"titled with the
    takeaway not just a description of what is graphed."**
13. **What not to include is as important as what to include.** Economise on words; use
    pictures and graphs; if you have a theorem, give an example; save proofs for the
    paper.
14. **"Assume you are talking to smart people but not experts. Think Journal of Economic
    Perspectives not Econometrica."**
15. Must be **readable from the back of the room**.
16. **Result tables: focus on the coefficient(s) of interest**; rows "Control variables?
    Yes / State fixed effects? Yes"; full tables in appendix slides.
17. Little eye candy (especially for a job talk). PowerPoint is fine; "the cool kids use
    Beamer for job talks, maybe you should too."
18. **Before the talk:** ask how long the seminar is; know the audience; send slides in
    advance; bring slides on USB and have them online (he emails them to himself). "It is
    YOUR talk. Take property rights over anything attached to it."
19. **Day of:** arrive early; check clicker, formatting, chalk/markers, water; be ready to
    have slides printed. "Do not waste your audience's time."
20. Face the audience; know your slides at a glance; **"You are telling the story of your
    research not reading slides."**
21. Be aware of verbal tics, pacing (mix fast/slow, loud/soft), where you stand: "move
    but do not pace"; no hands in pockets; don't hide in the corner. "Assume the Steve Jobs
    posture."
22. **"Do not let the audience dominate you!"** Practice so a question doesn't throw you
    off the story. "Every dept has at least one person who asks the same question at
    every seminar." Acceptable answers: "Good point, I'll look at that." / "Let me hold off
    on that until I get a bit further." / "I don't know. Let's discuss afterwards."
23. When answering, avoid side-discussions; speak to the whole audience. "Presentations in
    economics can be a combat sport!"
24. **Convey your excitement.** "If you are not excited about your research no one else
    will be."

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Yes — explicitly an economics seminar / job-talk guide (applied empirical slant: data,
identification, coefficient of interest). Gives the clearest standard applied-econ talk
order of the ten sources. Further reading it lists: Zettelmeyer, Shapiro, Bellemare,
Piazzesi.

## Conflicts with common advice

- Hook gets **five minutes** (Peyton Jones: 2 minutes; Winston: 5 minutes for vision +
  "done something"; Li: ≤3 of 20 minutes to reach the model).
- Preview of results marked **optional** (Shapiro, Bellemare, EUI: do it; Cochrane: don't).
- Deflects questions quickly to protect the story, vs. Meager (engage, try twice) and
  Peyton Jones (questions are the point).
