# Peyton Jones — How to Give a Great Research Talk

- Author: Simon Peyton Jones (Microsoft Research, Cambridge), based on a 1993 SIGPLAN
  Notices paper with John Hughes and John Launchbury
- Title: "How to give a good/great research talk"
- Year: 1993 paper; slides revised repeatedly (handout version c. 2000s; UCY copy is the
  later version that drops the overhead-projector slides and adds a "Technology" slide)
- URLs used: https://john.cs.olemiss.edu/~hcc/researchMethods/notes/PeytonJones/Giving_talk_handout.pdf
  (8 pp., 6-up handout) and https://www2.cs.ucy.ac.cy/courses/EPL670/docs/GivingATalk.pdf
  (38 slides). The microsoft.com page only links to the slides and paper.
- Length: ~36 slides

## Concrete rules

1. **"Your paper = the beef. Your talk = the beef advertisement. Do not confuse the two."**
2. The purpose of the talk is **not** to impress with brainpower, tell all you know, or
   present all technical details; it **is** to give an intuitive feel for the idea, "make
   them foam at the mouth with eagerness to read your paper", engage/excite/provoke.
3. Assume the actual audience: never heard of you, wish they hadn't heard of your
   formalism, "have just had lunch and are ready for a doze". **"Your mission is to WAKE
   THEM UP."**
4. **What to put in: 1. Motivation (20%). 2. Your key idea (80%). 3. There is no 3.**
5. **"You have 2 minutes to engage your audience before they start to doze."** Motivation
   answers: why tune in? what is the problem? why is it interesting?
6. **Identify one key idea**: "If the audience remembers only one thing from your talk,
   what should it be?" "What I did this summer" is No Good. Say it explicitly: "If you
   remember nothing else, remember this." Organise the talk around it and ruthlessly prune
   anything irrelevant to it.
7. **"Narrow, deep beats wide, shallow."** Avoid shallow overviews at all costs; cut to
   the technical meat.
8. **Examples are your main weapon** — to motivate, convey intuition, show the idea in
   action, extreme cases, shortcomings. **"When time is short, omit the general case, not
   the example."**
9. **No outline slide at the start** ("conveys near zero information"). Maybe an outline
   for orientation *after* the motivation, and signposts at pause points.
10. **Do not present related work.** But know it cold and answer questions readily;
    acknowledge co-authors on the title slide and precursors as you go; praise the
    opposition ("X's very interesting work does Y; I have extended it to do Z").
11. **Omit technical details**: "dense clouds of notation will send your audience to
    sleep". Present specific aspects only; refer to the paper; keep **backup slides for
    questions**.
12. **Do not apologise** ("I didn't have time to prepare", "my computer broke", "I don't
    have time to tell you about this", "I don't feel qualified").
13. **Write (or at least polish) the slides the night before** so the talk is fresh.
14. **Enthusiasm is your most potent weapon**: "If you do not seem excited by your idea,
    why should the audience be?"
15. Nerves ("the jelly effect"): breathe deeply during the previous talk, **script your
    first few sentences precisely**, move around and use large gestures, go to the loo
    first. "You are not a wimp. Everyone feels this way."
16. Point at the screen; speak to someone at the back; **make eye contact, identify a
    nodder** and speak to them.
17. **Questions are "a golden golden golden opportunity"**: encourage them during the
    talk, pause now and then and ask for questions. **Be prepared to truncate the talk**
    — "better to connect, and not to present all your material".
18. Don't reveal bullets one by one unless there is a punch line; use animation "very
    very ... sparingly".
19. **"Absolutely without fail, finish on time."** Audiences stop listening when the time
    is up. Simply truncate and conclude; **never ask "would you like me to go on?"**
20. Technology: test the laptop with the projector in advance; backup on the web and a
    USB key; borrow a laser pointer but avoid using it.
21. "The general standard is so low that you don't have to be outstanding to stand out."
    You attend 50× as many talks as you give: watch them intelligently.
22. Give a talk about any idea, "no matter how weedy and insignificant it may seem".

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Written for CS/PL conference talks (20–30 min, no interruptions), so the 20/80 split and
"omit the general case" assume a short slot. The one-key-idea, examples-first,
no-outline/no-lit-review, finish-on-time rules transfer directly; the "truncate rather
than present everything" rule is the right response to a question-heavy econ seminar.

## Conflicts with common advice

- **No outline slide at the start** vs. Bellemare, EUI, Tabarrok ("roadmap"/"plan of the
  presentation") and Fatahalian (section slides).
- Omits related work entirely, vs. EUI/Tabarrok "brief discussion of antecedents".
- "Write slides the night before" vs. everyone else's rehearse-repeatedly-with-a-timer.
- Encourages questions mid-talk (CS norm); econ seminars get them whether or not you ask.
