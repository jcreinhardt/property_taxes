# Shengwu Li — How to give an economic theory talk

- Author: Shengwu Li (Harvard)
- Title: "How to give an economic theory talk"
- Year: 4 March 2023
- URL used: https://sangmino.github.io/Documents/r45.pdf (copy; the original circulates
  from Li's Harvard page)
- Length: 4 pp., 13 numbered rules with sub-points; "mainly intended for graduate
  students"; "rules-of-thumb; I follow them except when there is a strong reason".

## Concrete rules

1. **Remember the curse of knowledge.** You've thought about this for months; the
   audience for minutes. Explain new concepts and unusual steps, even those trivial in
   hindsight ("the revelation principle was trivial only in hindsight").
   **Target audience: the second-year grad student in your field, not the Nobel laureate
   in the corner** — aiming there "will calibrate about right for faculty".
2. **Teaching, not advocacy.** Help the audience understand motivation, model, results,
   "discussing candidly both strengths and limitations". "Think of yourself like an
   astronomer describing a new exoplanet, not a real estate agent describing a house. If
   you act like you are selling something, the audience will treat you with suspicion."
   **"Strive to be the most informative person in the room about the limitations of the
   model and the results."**
3. **Get to the model quickly.** Long motivation lets the audience form opinions about
   what the model *should* be, then be disappointed. **Do not give the literature review
   before the model.** **Time to model: ≤3 minutes of a 20-minute talk; 7 of 45; 10 of
   75.**
4. **Slides enhance the voice, they don't replace it.** Three purposes only: notes for the
   audience (short bullets), material better seen than heard (graphs, tables, game trees,
   equations), material that must be exact (definitions, theorems). Plan what to say at
   each slide; **less than half of what you say should be on the slides**; "slides are
   not a teleprompter"; short, simple sentences; **font is right only if it looks too
   large on your computer**.
5. **Practice** in front of a mirror or a friend. Building slides catches errors ("like
   compiling code"). **Time yourself and cut until the talk fits at ordinary conversation
   speed with generous allowance for questions.**
6. **Work hard on graphic design.** Over-filled slides aren't read. High-contrast
   statements are believed more (Reber & Schwarz 1999); large fonts are remembered better
   (Ball et al. 2014). Use a consistent colour scheme (e.g. new definitions blue, key
   parts of expressions red); avoid red/green contrasts.
7. **Concrete examples before abstract statements.** Skipping the example because the
   theorem entails it is "a false economy". The example is a **unit test** for the
   audience's understanding of the assumptions. Choose a simple but non-trivial instance
   in which "nearby" misunderstandings are false (e.g. interim-IC but not ex-post-IC).
8. **The audience can remember at most seven pieces of notation.** Delete notation used
   fewer than three times; re-explain a symbol that hasn't appeared for several slides
   ("the arrival rate λ").
9. **Talk notation ≠ paper notation**: it must be aurally distinct (not a, A, script A).
   Use colour to make equations readable.
10. **Every equation on a slide is meant to be read: pause** longer than you think; walk
    through it. "If you ever say 'don't look at the equation, what really matters is...',
    then you should have deleted that equation." Never show equations to signal the proof
    was hard — "your audience already knows you can do algebra".
11. **Make precise and imprecise claims distinguishable.** "Theorem/proposition/lemma"
    only for statements that are precise, true and proved; label "informal result",
    "conjecture"; put undefined conditions in quotes ("for every 'nice' distribution")
    and say whether 'nice' will be defined.
12. Beamer makes equations easy and design hard; PowerPoint the reverse. "If you have
    enough equations that Beamer is easier to use, you have too many equations."
13. **Present a few things well rather than many badly.** **Rule of thumb: each slide
    takes two minutes** (excluding transitions). **Cut content rather than rush** —
    rushed content isn't remembered. **"Nobody will be unhappy if your talk ends slightly
    early."**

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Yes — written for econ theory seminars (20/45/75-minute formats), but the curse-of-
knowledge calibration, model-first timing, seven-symbol budget, example-as-unit-test,
two-minutes-per-slide and cut-don't-rush rules apply to any econ talk with a model or an
estimating equation.

## Conflicts with common advice

- **Not selling** — direct opposite of Meager's "you are in sales"; also at odds with
  Peyton Jones's "make them foam at the mouth".
- **Two minutes per slide** vs. Bellemare's one per minute.
- **Model within 3 minutes, no lit review before it** vs. Bellemare/Tabarrok hook +
  antecedents + roadmap; Tabarrok allows 5 minutes for the hook alone.
- Pause on every equation vs. Shapiro's "no pauses".
- Favours PowerPoint/Keynote over Beamer, vs. Bellemare and Tabarrok.
