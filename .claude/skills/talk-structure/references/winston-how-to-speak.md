# Winston — How to Speak

- Author: Patrick Winston (MIT CSAIL, d. 2019)
- Title: "How to Speak" (MIT OpenCourseWare RES.TLL-005, IAP lecture)
- Year: January 2018 recording (lecture given annually for ~40 years)
- URL used: official OCW transcript PDF,
  https://www.ocw.mit.edu/courses/15-371-innovation-teams-fall-2024/Unzc731iCUY_transcript.pdf
  (course page https://ocw.mit.edu/how_to_speak). Read in full (16 pp.).
- Length: 60-minute lecture; ~9,000-word transcript

## Concrete rules

1. Success is determined by "your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the
   quality of your ideas, in that order." Quality of speaking = **Knowledge × Practice ×
   (small) Talent** — technique can be learned.
2. **Rule of engagement: no laptops, no phones** — "we humans only have one language
   processor."
3. **Don't open with a joke** (people are still settling and not adjusted to your voice).
   **Open with an empowerment promise**: what they will know at the end that they don't
   know now.
4. Four heuristics: **cycle** on the idea (say it three times — "at any given moment
   about 20% of you will be fogged out"); **build a fence** around it so it isn't
   confused with someone else's ("mine is like Jones's algorithm except his is
   exponential and mine is linear"); **verbal punctuation** — enumerate, announce
   landmarks so listeners can "get back on the bus"; **ask a question** and wait —
   "I counted seven seconds" — neither too obvious nor too hard.
5. Time and place: 11 a.m. is best; **keep the room lights full up** ("it's extremely
   hard to see slides through closed eyelids"); case the room beforehand; a room more
   than half full.
6. Board for *informing* (teaching), slides for *exposing* (job talks, conference talks).
   The board's virtues: graphics, **speed matched to absorption**, a target for your
   hands. Props are remembered ("empathetic mirroring").
7. Slides: **"There are always too many slides, always too many words."** Slides are
   "condiments to what you're saying, not the main event."
8. Slide crimes and fixes: don't read slides; stand near the screen (avoid the tennis
   match); remove background junk, logos, titles, bullets, clutter; **minimum legible
   font ≈ 40–50 pt — "35 is beginning to get too small"** because it lets you cram words.
9. **No laser pointer** (you turn your back; "we could all leave and he wouldn't know");
   put an arrow on the slide instead.
10. **The too-heavy test**: print the deck and lay it on a table — is there air, white
    space, imagery? Text-only slides are allowed when historically significant, and you
    then give the audience time to read.
11. **Hapax legomenon**: one deliberately incomprehensible slide per talk, at most.
12. Oral exams fail for two reasons: **failure to situate** (context: a problem pursued
    worldwide, no progress in 30 years, impact on other things) and **failure to
    practise** — with friends who *don't* know the work, because advisors "hallucinate
    that there's material in your presentation that isn't there". "If you can't make me
    cry, I won't value you as a friend anymore."
13. **Job talk: in five minutes you must show (a) a vision and (b) that you've done
    something.** "If you haven't ... in five minutes, you've already lost." Vision = a
    problem somebody cares about + something new in your approach. "Done something" =
    enumerate the steps needed to realise the vision (not all need be done) and
    demonstrate.
14. Middle = "blah blah blah"; **conclude by enumerating your contributions** — the talk
    is a sandwich (steps at the start, contributions at the end).
15. Being remembered — **Winston's star**: a **Symbol**, a **Slogan** ("one-shot
    learning"), a **Surprise**, a **Salient idea** (one that sticks out — "some theses
    have too many good ideas"), a **Story** of how you did it and why it matters.
16. **Final slide = "Contributions"**, left up during questions. Not: a collaborator list
    (put it on the *first* slide), not "Questions?", not "Thank you", not "The End", not
    contact details, not "Conclusions" ("perfectly legitimate conclusions that nobody
    cares about").
17. **Final words: don't end with "thank you"** ("a weak move" — implies they stayed out
    of politeness). End with a joke (now they're ready for it), a benediction-style
    close, or **salute the audience** ("it's been great to see what you're doing here
    ... I look forward to coming back").

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Partly. It is a general lecture for MIT students (AI/CS), so the board-vs-slides and
inspiring-students material is for teaching. The job-talk rules (vision + done-something
in 5 minutes, contributions slide, situate, practise with strangers), the slide-text
rules, cycling/verbal punctuation, and the ending rules transfer directly to an econ job
talk; nothing on handling hostile questions or interruptions.

## Conflicts with common advice

- **Ending**: no "thank you", no "Questions?", end on *contributions* — vs. Fatahalian
  (end on broad future-looking note). Agrees with Bellemare (no Questions?/Thank you
  slide) and Winston goes further by banning the spoken thank-you.
- **No slide titles, no bullets**, 40–50 pt fonts — vs. Fatahalian/Tabarrok (title =
  the point) and EUI (24 pt, 15 lines).
- **Don't practise with your advisor** — others say rehearse with anyone.
- **Say it three times** (cycling) vs. Cochrane ("don't repeat things").
- Prefers chalk and props to slides; econ seminars are slide-driven.
