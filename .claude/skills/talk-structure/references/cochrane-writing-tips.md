# Cochrane — Writing Tips for PhD Students (seminar section + transferable writing rules)

- Author: John H. Cochrane (Chicago GSB at the time)
- Title: "Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students"
- Year: 8 June 2005
- URL used: https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/phd_paper_writing.pdf (the chicagobooth.edu
  link returns 404)
- Length: 13 pp.; §4 "Seminar presentations" is ~1.2 pp.; §1 "Organization" and §2
  "Writing" supply the rules below that transfer to slides

## Concrete rules — §4 Seminar presentations (complete)

1. **"You will not believe how fast the time will go by."** Time is limited and "we
   can't skim to the important stuff in a seminar", so get to the point.
2. **"You don't need any literature review or motivation in a seminar. Just get to the
   point."** Model: Gene Fama starts seminars with "Look at table 1."
3. **"Don't 'preview' results. It wastes time; why say it twice rather than say it once?"**
4. **Don't make slides with a bullet point for every word you intend to say** — it locks
   you into a preplanned order so you can't adapt as time runs out.
5. Slides that contain **only equations, tables and graphs** are fine. Use words at most
   for the one or two things people must remember, e.g. "Identification: interest rates
   do not respond to fiscal shocks in the Ricardian model."
6. People need to see the model structure and variable definitions; too much junk means
   "people can't see the utility function while you're talking about the production
   function". **"People don't remember equations from one slide to the next."**
7. **"You have to leave slides up for a decent amount of time ... That means you will not
   be able to put up 1 slide per minute!"**
8. Main objective, as in the paper: **get to the #1 contribution as fast as possible.**
9. Anatomy of the disaster seminar: pointless motivation and policy implications (can't
   be followed before the result) → long literature review → a results preview that
   provokes "dumb" questions because the claims have no documentation yet → beside-the-
   point "theory" → distracting preliminary results → tangents → with 10 minutes left the
   speaker tells people to be quiet and rushes the main results. "I timed the finance
   workshop last winter quarter and not one paper got to the main results in under an
   hour!"
10. **Listen to questions all the way to the end, then count to three before answering.**
    "This isn't a game show"; often you don't actually know what the question will be.
11. **Keep a sheet of paper handy** — some questions point to changes for the paper.
12. **"You cannot make it too simple."** PhD students overestimate how much theory people
    can digest in one sitting and how fast they memorise models and results.
13. Speak loudly, slowly and clearly.
14. **"There's nothing wrong with ending early!"**

## Writing rules that transfer to slide text (§1–2)

15. **Figure out the one central and novel contribution; write it in one concrete
    paragraph.** Not "many interesting results" but what the result is (Fama–French 1992
    abstract as model).
16. **"Triangular / newspaper style, not joke / novel style"**: punchline first, then
    explain. "Readers don't stick around to find the punchline in Table 12." Most student
    papers *and workshop presentations* get this wrong: the contribution appears "in the
    last 5 minutes of the seminar".
17. **Not a travelogue**: "We don't care how you came to figure out the right answer ...
    Save it for your memoirs."
18. Introduction: start with the contribution, give the fact behind it ("in a regression
    of x on y, controlling for z, the coefficient is q"). No "clearing your throat" (no
    philosophy, no "the literature has long been interested", no long policy motivation).
19. Literature review *after* the contribution, set off so it can be skipped; its job is
    to set the paper against **the 2 or 3 closest papers** and give credit.
20. **"There should be nothing before the main result that a reader does not need to know
    in order to understand the main result."** Theory = the minimum needed for the
    empirics; work out only the specialised model you take to data.
21. Start empirics with the main result; no warm-ups, data description of well-known
    datasets, preliminary estimates or failed specifications. Then intuition
    graphs/tables, then limited robustness (most of it to an appendix).
22. Conclusions: short; don't restate all findings; no grant application of future work;
    don't speculate.
23. "Every word must count"; "Do I really have to say this?"; don't repeat; previews and
    recalls ("as we will see in Table 6") signal poor ordering.
24. Tables: self-contained caption with the regression equation and variable names; **no
    number in a table that isn't discussed**; 2–3 significant digits; sensible units.
25. Figures: label axes, define every symbol in the caption, line types that survive
    reproduction.
26. **"Keep track of what your reader knows and doesn't know. Most Ph.D. students assume
    far too much."** Say what you do, then explain, then compare to alternatives — in
    that order, at micro and macro level.
27. Active voice, "I" on sole-authored work, present tense, subject–verb–object, short
    words ("use" not "utilize"), no adjectives on your own work ("striking", "very
    significant"), delete everything before "that", clothe the naked "this".
28. Name Greek letters and remind readers of the name ("the elasticity of substitution θ")
    — "the one place where a little repetition isn't bad".
29. Strike "I leave x for future research"; never "illustrative" empirical work.
30. Don't "assume" model structure — just state it; save "assume" for things that
    restrict the real world.

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Written for finance/econ PhD students; §4 is explicitly about the 90-minute Chicago-style
seminar with interruptions. The result-first order is the sharpest form of "preview"
advice: skip the preview *and* the motivation and show the main table.

## Conflicts with common advice

- **No motivation, no literature, no preview** — contradicts Shapiro, Tabarrok, Bellemare,
  EUI (hook + preview) and Peyton Jones (20% motivation). Nobody else says "Look at
  table 1."
- **Fewer than one slide per minute**, vs. Bellemare's "roughly one per minute" (Li's
  two minutes per slide and EUI's 12 per hour agree with Cochrane).
- Slides of pure equations/tables with almost no words, vs. Fatahalian's point-as-title.
- "Count to three before answering" vs. Tabarrok's quick deflection.
