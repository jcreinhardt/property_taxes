# Meager — Public Speaking for Academic Economists

- Author: Rachael Meager (LSE at the time)
- Title: "Public Speaking for Academic Economists"
- Year: 24 October 2017 (slide deck for a PhD seminar)
- URL used: http://www.princeton.edu/~reddings/tradephd/public_speaking_for_academic_economists.pdf
- Length: 17 slides, ~290 lines of text

## Concrete rules

1. **"Good ideas do not sell themselves."** Once you stand in front of people who don't
   know or believe what you do, "you are in sales and/or marketing". Accept it.
2. You cannot sell an idea without understanding **what the audience cares about and how
   they think**; what convinces you may not convince them.
3. **The talk is about them, not you**: not what you wanted, thought, did, but what they
   want, think, might do. Quote (Mike Monteiro): "You don't sell a house by talking about
   sheetrock. You sell it by getting the buyer to picture themselves in the neighborhood."
4. **Look at and talk to the audience. Be loud enough. Be slow enough.** "If you can't say
   everything you need to say when you are talking slowly, you are trying to say too
   much."
5. Use language they understand; **if you must use jargon, explain it**.
6. **"Your first job is to inspire confidence." Do not start with an apology.**
7. Do not pace around the room (looks nervous, cuts you off from the audience). Move
   hands naturally, as in a conversation you care about. **Do not stand in front of your
   slides.**
8. **Slides: as little information as possible, conditional on conveying the point at a
   glance.** Never write anything the audience cannot read.
9. **Graphs > tables.** In tables, never display irrelevant numbers; slide tables look
   different from paper tables.
10. **Own the room.** You are responsible for what happens in your talk; it doesn't matter
    that the audience is more accomplished. "People will forgive committed foolishness.
    They will not forgive you for not taking the seminar — or their time — seriously."
11. **Questions are why you are there**; they tell you what the audience cares about.
12. **Don't interrupt or assume you know the question.** Demonstrate active listening.
13. Avoid being dismissive: find what is underneath the question and speak to that.
14. You may say "I don't think we'll resolve this now, let's discuss offline" — but only
    **after trying to resolve it twice**.
15. **A question is not a request for change.** ("Why did you cluster at the state
    level?" — "I can change it!" is wrong.) The asker wants your reasoning; immediate
    capitulation suggests you haven't thought about it. Give a **1-sentence explanation**,
    not your life story.
16. **Don't get defensive.** "You are not your work, and your work is not you." If you
    cannot defend without getting defensive, concede; "I need to think about that" is fine.
17. **Don't assume people know what you know.** Grad students "vastly overestimate what
    the audience knows and how much information the audience can absorb". Spell out what
    now seems obvious to you.
18. People must feel they **understand your work well enough to critique it**, or they
    won't buy it.
19. **Don't try to show how hard you worked** — boring, signals disrespect for their time.
    Frame it so the idea feels obvious now, and remind them why it wasn't obvious before.
20. **Present the work you have, not the work you wish you had.** "By the time you are
    standing in front of a room, the amount of work you have done is the exact right
    amount." Don't tell them what you couldn't finish (brown bags are a slight exception,
    but still mostly present what is done or concrete plans).
21. **Stay loose.** Confidence ≠ perfection. Expect a few dumb mistakes every
    presentation; sell on advantages, admit flaws where necessary; you control only what
    you do.

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Yes — written for economics PhD students, with brown-bag and seminar examples (clustering
question). Covers delivery and Q&A more than structure; says nothing about talk order,
time budget, or slide counts.

## Conflicts with common advice

- Frames the talk as **selling**; Shengwu Li says the opposite ("teaching, not advocacy
  ... if you act like you are selling something, the audience will treat you with
  suspicion"). Meager's version: sell honestly, by explaining why *you* believe it.
- "Try twice before deferring offline" is more generous to askers than Tabarrok/EUI,
  who defer quickly to protect the story.
- Says nothing about enthusiasm as a tool (Peyton Jones, Tabarrok, Bellemare, Winston).
