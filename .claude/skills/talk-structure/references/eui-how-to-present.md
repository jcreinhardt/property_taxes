# EUI — How to Present

- Authors: Massimo Motta and Karl Schlag, Department of Economics, European University
  Institute
- Title: "How to Present (some ideas accumulated over time)"
- Year: 13 November 2000
- URL used: https://www.eui.eu/Documents/DepartmentsCentres/Economics/RulesForms/HowToPresent.pdf
- Length: 8 pp. of bullet points (overhead-projector era; "slides" = transparencies)

## Concrete rules

1. **Preparation:** spend ample time on the slides; **rehearse repeatedly and aloud**
   (ideally with somebody); practise pronouncing cited names; check timing — not too
   fast, not too slow.
2. **Allow buffer time: questions and discussion "might take a lot of precious time."**
   Bring a watch; ask the chair to warn you five minutes before the end.
3. Find out who the audience is (economics faculty, students, political scientists).
4. **"They will fall asleep within 5 minutes if you do not catch their interest."**
5. **"They can only remember a few things: let them exit the seminar remembering the
   punch-line of the paper."**
6. Check everyone can see the slide, hear you, see the screen.
7. Appearance: dress nicely ("one cannot overdress"); don't walk around all the time;
   don't turn to the blackboard; hands out of pockets; no tics; look at several listeners,
   **not only the advisor/friend**.
8. Thank for the invitation. **Speak slow, loud and clear — "if time is running out try
   to speak even slower."**
9. **Stop briefly between slides or sections; make occasional long stops**; invite
   questions before a new section ("it is now a good moment to ask questions"); pause to
   give examples and intuition.
10. **Slide budget: max 12 slides for 1 h (max 15 for 1h15); "each counts, long ones
    count double."**
11. Prepare **additional slides for questions**.
12. **Prepare short-cuts in case you run out of time** (post-it notes marking where to
    cut).
13. If you go to the blackboard, plan it and count it as a slide. Don't flash slides;
    link slides verbally ("I am now going to show the implication..."); **follow the
    order on the slide** (don't jump from line 4 to line 1 and skip 2–3).
14. **Font ≥ 24 pt; max 15 lines per slide**, few concepts per slide (or spend more time
    and count it double). Colours help but don't overdo; consistent fonts; bullets.
15. Figures and graphs: useful but must be legible. **Large tables: the audience will
    look at every entry — mark in colour or circle where to focus.**
16. Avoid complete sentences; citations only if very short; no non-standard
    abbreviations.
17. **Contents in order:** cover (title, authors, affiliations, date) → **introduction,
    motivation, anticipation of results (punch-line)** → plan of the presentation →
    literature (only relevant pieces; similarities/differences; "avoid spending hours on
    previous literature") → the model → main results → policy implications / examples →
    **conclusion (punch-line = take-home, max 4 results)** → future research.
18. People should leave knowing **what is new/original** in your paper.
19. **"Do not overestimate audience: if you are too technical, you might lose 90% of
    audience from minute 1"** — but don't be so loose that they doubt you know your
    techniques; layer the presentation so both types profit.
20. Applied paper: give policy implications if any, but don't make them up. Theory paper:
    explain via examples or special cases the audience knows; cite stylised facts that
    support the thesis.
21. Model: say whether it follows/departs from known models; don't forget timing and
    information; formally correct definitions; justify assumptions when needed but
    "do not invite all possible generalizations".
22. **Results: first state formally, then explain the intuition.** Proofs: intuition and
    technique only — "explicit proofs are almost never important".
23. Questions: anticipate them when practising; **"few questions during talk = bad sign"**;
    listen; repeat in your own words; **short answers**; answer comprehension questions
    immediately; defer extensions and niche interests; never present unproven results as
    proven.
24. Be polite but firm with out-of-scope or insistent questioners: defer to the end or to
    coffee. If you don't know, say so (then maybe reason aloud). Avoid "nice question" +
    pivoting to your own work; don't spend time on questions about previous literature.
25. **Present a special case and say the paper has the general setting.**
26. Don't explain indirectly ("as we know") — that assumes listeners are stupid.
27. No miracle results. Impress with the take-home message and clarity, "not on how
    complicated [the] issue is".
28. Ask others afterwards how you did; never ask interviewers "so, how did I go?"

## Applies to economics seminar / job talk?

Yes — an economics department's official guidance, written with theory papers and the
job market in mind (model, results, proofs, "interviewers"). The 12-slides-per-hour and
15-lines/24-pt limits reflect transparencies but the slide-time budget is still the most
conservative benchmark among the sources.

## Conflicts with common advice

- **12 slides per hour** vs. Bellemare ~1/minute (Cochrane and Li side with EUI).
- Includes a **literature slide** and **future research** at the end (Peyton Jones,
  Cochrane, Fatahalian variously object).
- **Invite questions between sections** and treat few questions as a bad sign, vs.
  Tabarrok's keep-the-audience-from-dominating.
- Dress and blackboard rules are era-specific.
